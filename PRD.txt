Product Requirements Document — "Forest" (森林)
A private garden that grows from what you actually do — one tree for every interest you keep alive, planted for a rural-China classroom and built to work for anyone.

Last revised: 2026-09-12. Section numbers are cited from code comments and migrations (e.g. "PRD §9") — renumber only with a matching pass over those references.


1. Vision
Open the app and see a garden. Every tree in it is something you love — Piano, Basketball, Watercolours — and every tree only grows because you actually went and did the thing. Log ten minutes of practice, water the tree. Skip too many days, the tree wilts, and eventually dies. Bring it back to life if you want to, but it costs you something to do it.

The garden is private by default. Nobody sees it unless you turn on "let others find me," or unless you're in the same class as the person looking. Next to the garden sits a second world — Explore — where a student can see hobby ideas curated for them, find classmates who share their interests, and read or post to a small, low-stakes community feed. A student earns coins for logging real activity and spends them on hair, outfits, and hobby badges for their avatar — the only thing money buys in this app is you, cosmetically, never an advantage or a shortcut around the core loop.

It competes with a phone's default gravity — games, short video — not by matching their loop-closing tricks beat for beat, but by pointing the same mechanics (streaks, unlocks, a small reward waiting for you) at things a student actually made or practised in the real world.


2. Problem
Two problems, not one:

Motivation decays faster than interest does. A student picks up a hobby, does it for two weeks, and then life gets busy — homework, a slow week, no one around to practise with — and the habit quietly dies. Nothing in their day reminds them the hobby, or their progress in it, still exists.

Rural students have a thinner support structure for sustaining a hobby. Fewer teachers who can specialise in a given interest, fewer peers doing the same thing, less disposable income for lessons or equipment, and long unstructured hours that default to a phone. The gap between "wants to keep doing this" and "actually does" is wider here than for a well-resourced student.

Low-effort dopamine is not the enemy exactly — it's winning by default because it's frictionless and immediately rewarding. This app's bet is that if real activity is made just as frictionless to log and just as immediately rewarding to see grow, it can compete for the same idle attention.


3. Target users & account types
Two account types exist, and they are not symmetric — they are two different apps sharing a shell.

3.1 Individual / student account
Secondary-school students, roughly 13–18. Originally framed for rural Jiangxi students; nothing about the product requires that context, and it works as a general hobby tracker for anyone.
Primary device: phone, shared or personal. Mobile-first responsive web.
Signs up with a username and password only — no email. Some students don't have one, so an address is derived from the username (see §7.6) purely to satisfy the auth provider; it is never reachable.
Lands on their own Home — the garden.

3.2 Org / educator account
A teacher or school running a class. Signs up with a real email, since only they need one.
Does not get a garden. Their Home is a dashboard: a class code to hand out, a roster, and rollup stats.
Skips the parts of onboarding that only make sense for someone logging their own hobbies.
A class code is minted automatically at the end of onboarding, rather than typed in.

3.3 How the two connect
A student joins a class by entering the code their teacher hands out, from Me → Join a class. Joining sets their class_code, which unlocks the School tab in Explore and places them on that educator's roster. Both the roster and the classmate list are live queries against real accounts.

Joining is validated server-side by join_class(), a security-definer function. This matters: the classes table is readable only by the educator who owns each row, so a code cannot be discovered by listing them — it has to be handed to you. A student cannot write their own class_code directly, and cannot change their own account_type (see §9 and §7.1).


4. Goals & non-goals

Goals (current build)
Let a student create interests ("trees") and see them all at a glance, as a garden.
Let a student log real activity against an interest — a journal entry with a duration, a photo, a voice note, or any combination — in well under a minute.
Make the consequence of not showing up visible: a tree that isn't tended visibly wilts, and can die. This is the central point of difference from a plain streak counter — neglect has a visible cost, not just a broken number.
Forgive one bad day. A rest day bridges a single missed day per rolling week so one missed Tuesday doesn't erase six weeks of work, while an every-other-day pattern still breaks.
Reward real logging with a light in-app currency, spendable only on cosmetics — never on anything that changes the core loop's difficulty or outcome.
Keep everything private unless the student opts in, at the account level, and let individual entries and photos be shared more narrowly than that.
Give a student low-stakes ways to find their next interest: curated ideas, classmates' interests, and a small community feed.
Give a teacher a zero-setup way to stand up a class: one code, a dashboard, nothing else to configure.
Work reliably offline for the core loop via local-first storage, with background sync and a visible retry path when sync falls behind.
Support English and Chinese as first-class, toggle-anytime languages throughout, including all curated content.
Let a student take it all back: erase the garden, or delete the account outright.

Non-goals — deliberately not built, and not planned
No direct messaging, no comments, no free-text reply between users. The riskiest feature class in any product aimed at minors, and not required for the value the app delivers.
No follower counts, public leaderboards, or like counts visible to others.
No follow-a-person primitive. Watching is scoped to a hobby, never to a user — see §9.
No infinite feed anywhere.
No real location, no school-identifying data, no contact details collected or shown.
No push notifications that require a server. Reminders are local, in-browser, opt-in.
No pay-to-win mechanic of any kind. Coins only ever buy appearance. Hard line, not a scoping decision.
No public global discovery across schools. Social surfaces are scoped to a class, or to accounts that explicitly opted into being findable.


5. The core loops
Three loops layered on top of each other. The first is the product; the other two feed it.

5.1 Cultivate (primary)
Open → see your garden at whatever health it's actually at → tap a tree → see its "why," recent photos and entries, its streak → log today's activity in well under a minute → watch the growth stage and health respond, and earn coins.

Hard quality bar, carried from the product's origins: logging must be fast. A student who has to fight the UI to record five minutes of piano practice will not do it on a day when they only have five minutes.

5.2 Tend (the stakes loop)
Every tree has a schedule — which weekdays the student intends to work on it — and a health state derived from how recently it was actually logged. Miss it long enough and the tree wilts; miss it longer and it dies. A dead tree can be revived by spending coins, which resets its decay clock. A warning appears before death, not after, since reviving costs something.

This is what makes the garden feel alive rather than decorative: the absence of activity is visible, not just its presence. It is also the loop that has to be tuned most carefully — too punishing and it becomes guilt, too forgiving and it stops mattering (§14).

5.3 Discover (secondary, deliberately small)
Open Explore → a short list of hobby ideas, some recommended from categories adjacent to what the student already does → optionally see classmates and what they're into → optionally read or post to a small community feed → tap an idea for a description → start a tree from it.

Explore is a finder, not a feed. Nothing in it is designed to be scrolled indefinitely; it ends in either "I'll try that" or "nothing today," both fine.


6. Screens & features

6.1 Home — the garden (individual accounts)
A swipeable wall of tree cards, one per interest, each rendered at a growth stage and health derived from its logging history.
A zoomed-out view showing the whole forest as a grid, for a garden too big to swipe through.
A resurfaced memory banner — a past photo or entry ("Remember this?"), the hook meant to pull a student back on days they weren't planning to open the app.
A nudge banner for something actionable: today is a scheduled day, or a tree needs attention.
A dying banner warning before a tree is lost, with how long is left.
A streak chip: days logged across every hobby, unified — logging any one tree keeps it alive. Shows a flame, or a leaf while a rest day is holding the chain together.
Trees change appearance with the real seasons.

6.2 Home — dashboard (org/educator accounts)
No garden. Their class code, front and centre, with one-tap copy.
Rollup stats: number of students, total hobbies.
A live roster; tapping a student opens a read-only sheet.

6.3 Interest detail — a tree's page
Header: name, colour, "why I love this," and the tree itself at its current stage and health.
Journal tab — dated, reverse-chronological entries, each with a duration, optional text, and an optional voice note. Entries can be edited or deleted.
Album tab — a photo grid; tap for full size. A photo can be pinned as the tree's cover.
Per-item sharing: entries and photos each carry their own visibility. There is no longer a per-tree public/private toggle — that collapsed into the account-level setting in §6.6, because two settings for one idea was confusing and easy to set inconsistently.
Growth replay — a short animation of every stage the tree has grown through.
Stats: hours, photos, entries, and this tree's own streak.
Reminder schedule, editable per tree.
Revive action once a tree has died.
Delete is undoable via a toast, and lands in Recently Deleted for 30 days.

6.4 Public interest view — someone else's tree, read-only
Reached at /user/:userId/interest/:interestId. Shows what that owner made public. No edit affordances.
From here a viewer can start the same hobby themselves — which creates their own separate tree, never joins the owner's — or keep an eye on this tree (§6.6), or report it.

6.5 Explore
Ideas tab (individual accounts): a personalised "For you" section recommending categories adjacent to what the student already does, with a plain-language reason; plus the full curated list, shuffled and reshufflable. Ideas already taken never repeat. Every idea has a description in both languages and belongs to one of seven categories.
A user-search bar sits above the tabs, gated by the other account's own opt-in discoverable flag and enforced by row-level security.
Community tab: a small live feed of journal entries their authors explicitly shared. Finite, no comments, no infinite scroll. A safety note sits above it.
School tab (once a student has a class code): live classmates and the hobbies they share with you.
Every post, profile, tree, photo and entry reachable from here carries a report control, and every person carries a block control.

6.6 Profile ("Me")
Avatar preview, name, account stats.
Avatar customisation: skin tone and base hair/outfit colours are always free — identity shouldn't cost coins. Extra styles cost coins.
Language toggle, affecting the whole app immediately.
Theme: White, Black, or System.
Username change, checked server-side before committing.
"Let others find me" — opts the account into user search and into its trees being visible to non-classmates. Off by default. This is now the only account-level visibility control.
Join a class.
Badges, Year in Review, Memories, Recently Deleted, and the list of hobbies you're watching.
Sound effects toggle; reminder permission.
Clear all data — erases every tree, photo, entry and voice note, locally and on the server, keeping the account and profile. Reports whether the server half actually succeeded.
Delete my account — erases the account itself and everything under it, permanently. Two-tap, with the consequence stated before the confirming tap.

6.7 Market
Spends the coin economy on hobby-badge decorations, equippable on the avatar. Owned-but-unequipped items show an equip button; unaffordable items are disabled. Balance shown persistently.

6.8 Onboarding
Account type and username/password are chosen at signup, before onboarding begins.
Individual: intro → gender → interests → confirm → schedule → look → notifications.
Org: intro → look → notifications.
Gender only picks a starting hair style; it is never stored or shown as a demographic field, and is freely changeable afterwards. "Prefer not to say" keeps the shared default.
Interests are filtered — video-game and social-media names are rejected as hobbies, in both languages.
Finishing sets onboarding_completed, an explicit flag rather than an inference from whether a display name exists.

6.9 Shared interaction patterns
Undo toast on destructive actions, rather than a confirmation dialog on every delete.
Bottom sheets for nearly every add/edit interaction, in service of the under-a-minute bar.
Language toggle available from nearly every top bar.
A sync indicator appears only when something has been stuck for a while, with a manual retry.


7. Data model

7.1 users (Supabase)
id uuid — primary key, from auth
display_name text — unique, chosen at signup, changeable later
avatar text — JSON blob of the avatar's parts
bio text
account_type text — individual | org. Set once at signup from auth metadata; frozen afterwards by a trigger (§9)
discovery_enabled boolean — opts into user search and non-classmate visibility; off by default
class_code text — which class this account belongs to. Writable only through join_class()
onboarding_completed boolean
coins integer
owned_decorations, owned_hair, owned_outfits text[]
equipped_decoration text
lang, theme text; sound_on boolean
created_at timestamp

7.2 interests (a tree)
id text — client-generated, so an offline write doesn't wait on the server
user_id uuid — owner
name, why, color
days integer[] — which weekdays this tree expects activity
species, leaf_color — cosmetic
revived_at timestamp — set when a dead tree is revived; resets the decay clock
deleted_at timestamp — tombstone for Recently Deleted
created_at / updated_at

Dropped along the way: visibility (collapsed into the account-level setting), friends, category, inspired_by, time.

7.3 entries (a journal log)
id text, interest_id text, date text
text, minutes integer
visibility text — independent of anything above it
is_pinned boolean — eligible for the resurfaced memory banner
shared_to_feed boolean — this is what a community "post" is; there is no separate posts table, so a post can never drift from the entry it came from
audio_path, audio_ms — voice note in Storage
deleted_at, created_at / updated_at

7.4 photos
id text, interest_id text, storage_path text, caption, visibility, is_pinned, deleted_at, created_at.
Bytes live in a private Storage bucket, keyed by owner uid. The local copy is a Blob in IndexedDB; a photo not on this device is fetched lazily the first time something tries to display it.

7.5 classes, watches, blocks, reports
classes — one row per educator: their code and who owns it. Readable only by that owner.
watches — "keep an eye on this hobby." Points at an interest, never at a user; there is deliberately no follows table (§9).
blocks — (A, B) means A blocked B. Every visibility check tests both directions.
reports — target_type is interest | photo | entry | user. Insert-and-read-your-own only; no update policy exists, because changing a report's status is service-role work (§14).

7.6 Derived signup address
Individuals sign up with a username and no email. Supabase Auth requires an address, so one is derived deterministically from the username and never delivered. Login re-derives the same address rather than looking anything up, which makes "the same username always derives the same address" a correctness requirement. display_name's unique index is the real uniqueness gate.

7.7 Sync model
Local-first: every write lands in IndexedDB first and reflects immediately in the UI, then pushes to Supabase in the background. A failed push is tracked and retried — on a timer, when the browser regains a connection, and on demand — rather than being logged and forgotten. Anything stuck for long enough surfaces a quiet indicator with a retry.
On sign-in, a one-time reconciliation pass pushes local records not yet on the server and pulls down server records not cached locally.
Signing out wipes the local cache, so a shared device doesn't leak one student's garden to the next.

7.8 Derived, not stored
Growth stage, health, streaks and rest days, hour totals, the recommended Explore set, badges, and the resurfaced memory candidate are all computed at render time.


8. The coin economy & decay system
Earning: every logged entry or photo earns a fixed number of coins. This is the only faucet — no daily login bonus, nothing that decouples the reward from the activity it reinforces.
Spending: hobby-badge decorations and non-default hair/outfit styles. Every priced item is purely cosmetic.
Growth: five stages, each costing more logs than the last (5, then 6, 7, 8), so growth visibly slows as a tree gets bigger.
Decay: unattended trees move through wilting and bare, and die at thirty days idle. A warning appears in the final five.
Rest days: one missed day per rolling week is bridged rather than fatal. Rest days are not counted into the streak number — they only keep the chain connected.
Revival: a flat coin cost, which resets the decay clock. Fails cleanly if unaffordable.

Design tension to watch: a decay mechanic meant to motivate can read as punitive to a student who missed a week for exams, illness, or a hard week at home — context the app cannot see. This is the single biggest product risk in the system and is treated as such in §14.


9. Social, discovery & safety design
The social surface is intentionally much smaller and more constrained than a typical discover feature.

No direct messaging or comments anywhere.
No follow-a-person primitive. Watching points at a hobby; the schema is what stops "follow this child" being built by accident.
Community posts are journal entries their authors explicitly shared, one at a time.
Discoverability is opt-in and off by default, enforced by row-level security rather than hidden in the UI.
Visibility is granular down to the individual entry and photo.
A content filter rejects inappropriate freeform hobby names before they can exist.
Class scoping: social surfaces are scoped to a class code or to accounts that opted into being findable. There is no cross-class discovery.
Blocking is mutual disengagement, not a one-way mute: every visibility check tests both directions, so blocking someone also hides you from them.
Report and block are reachable from where the thing is actually looked at — a profile, a tree, a photo in the viewer, an individual entry, a feed post — not from a settings screen a distressed child has to go hunting for.

Privilege boundaries, enforced in the database and not the client:
A student cannot make themselves an educator. account_type is set once at signup and frozen by a trigger.
A student cannot join a class they weren't given the code to. The classes table is readable only by its owner, so codes cannot be enumerated, and class_code is writable only through join_class(), which checks the code server-side.

Explicitly out of scope as a hard line: DMs, public leaderboards, comments, location, real names, school-identifying fields, and any pay-to-win mechanic.


10. Tech stack & architecture
React 18, built with Vite 7. Routes are code-split; vendor bundles are split by what changes together.
Supabase — Postgres, auth, row-level security, and Storage. RLS is the enforcement point for everything visibility-related; the client is never trusted with it.
IndexedDB via Dexie for the local cache — deliberately not localStorage, whose size cap and inability to store binary blobs would rule out photos and voice notes.
React Router 7 — /, /interest/:id, /user/:userId/interest/:interestId, /explore, /profile, /market, with sheets layered on top rather than routed.
A custom I18nContext with a full English/Chinese string table covering UI copy, onboarding, and every curated content item.
PWA: manifest plus service worker.
Deployment: GitHub Pages via GitHub Actions, with a 404.html redirect so client-side deep links survive.
Reminders: browser Notification API, opt-in, scheduled client-side per tree.
Tests: Vitest, covering the derived logic that fails quietly — streaks and rest days, tree growth and death thresholds, date keys under a fixed non-UTC timezone, and the derived signup address.

Error handling: a root error boundary around the providers, and a second per-route boundary so one crashed screen doesn't take the navigation down with it.


11. Localization
English and Chinese (Simplified) are both fully supported, including all curated content — every hobby idea, category label and caption exists as an [en, zh] pair. The toggle is available from nearly every top bar and takes effect immediately across the whole app. The product is written to be used natively in Chinese, with English maintained as an equally real second language rather than a fallback.


12. Build status
Live and real: accounts, auth, sync, classes and rosters, the community feed, photo and voice-note storage, coins and purchases, report and block, watching a hobby, account deletion.
Fixture data remains in exactly one place: the demo classmate cards in StudentSheet. The roster and School tab are live; don't read the fixture's presence as evidence otherwise.
Removed along the way, deliberately: the demo garden, the guided tutorial, the export-my-data feature, the per-tree visibility toggle, the "who you do this with" field, and the daily learning goal.

Ahead of the original phased plan: accounts and sync, per-entry visibility, a second account type, the coin/decay/revival economy, avatar customisation, and two separate discovery surfaces were all either later-phase or absent from it.

Behind it: the safety operations layer. The report button exists now; the queue behind it does not (§14).


13. Success criteria
The core log-an-entry loop is fast on a real phone. Measured, not assumed — time an actual student adding a photo or entry from a cold open.
Decay feels motivating, not punishing, over a multi-week real-world trial including at least one student having a genuinely bad week. If the reaction to a dead tree is guilt rather than "let me revive that," the tuning is wrong regardless of how it demos.
A student's garden survives real conditions: closing the tab, losing wifi mid-session, switching devices, sharing a device with a sibling.
At least one real student uses it unprompted for over a week and can say, in their own words, what brought them back or what made them stop.
Visibility is provably correct: demonstrate live that a private entry is genuinely invisible to a second real account, not merely hidden by the UI.
A report filed by a child is seen by an adult within a defined window. This cannot be demonstrated today (§14).


14. Known limitations & open questions
Ranked by what stands between this and real children using it, not by difficulty.

No moderation queue. Reports can now be filed from five places and nobody can read any of them — there is no select policy beyond the reporter's own rows and no tooling anywhere. A child can report something and nothing happens. This is the most serious open item in the product and it needs a named human, not just a screen.

No age gate, privacy policy, terms of service, or parental consent flow. Nothing in the app asks how old anyone is. COPPA (under 13, US) and GDPR-K (under 16, EU) both assume these exist. This is a legal blocker, not a feature gap, and the policy text has to come from a lawyer.

No account recovery. A student signs up with no email, so there is no reset path — and there isn't one for educators with a real email either. A child who forgets their password loses their entire garden permanently. This needs a product decision about mechanism before it can be built.

No data export. It existed and was deliberately removed. Erasure without portability is half of what GDPR asks for, and a student, parent or teacher currently has no way to get a copy of a garden before it's gone.

Account deletion depends on an unverified grant. delete_my_account() deletes from auth.users, a schema this project does not own. It works on a normal Supabase project, but if the grant is absent it fails at runtime rather than at migration time — a legally required feature quietly broken while looking implemented. Verify against the live project; if it fails, move it to an edge function.

No automated image screening. Photos sync and are visible to classmates and to anyone the owner is discoverable to. Nothing scans them.

Decay tuning is unvalidated with real students under real stress. A product-design risk, not a numbers-tuning task.

Test coverage stops at pure logic. Streaks, growth, dates and the derived address are covered. Nothing covers RLS, sync, or any component — and RLS is where the real risk lives.

No error monitoring in production. You find out something broke when a child tells a teacher.

No screen reader support, and no bigger-text option.

Naming drift: the app is "Forest," the codebase still says "orbs" in places, and this document was titled "Leaves" until this revision. Worth one pass before new contributors inherit the confusion.
